# Manual Capture Friction

## Executive synthesis

Manual capture friction is useful when the goal is not to collect everything, but to collect the right things. In knowledge systems and AI workflows, a little effort at the moment of intake can improve judgment, reduce noise, and create more ownership over what gets stored. The mechanism is simple: a small cost, delay, or review step forces a keep-or-skip decision before something becomes part of the record. That helps curated notes, drafts, and rereading workflows. It hurts when the workflow needs to capture live information fast, because people will abandon anything that forces them to stop mid-task. The evidence is consistent across the sources, but it is mostly qualitative rather than measured.

## Example in practice

### A curation gate for captured notes

A team uses an AI note tool to collect ideas from meetings and chats. Instead of auto-saving every snippet into the permanent knowledge base, the tool shows each item in a review queue. The user has to keep it, edit it, or drop it before it is stored. This adds a little delay, but it prevents the system from filling up with low-value fragments and makes the saved corpus easier to trust later. The same pattern can work for drafts generated by an assistant: quick to create, but not automatically treated as finished work.

- Why it helps: It turns capture into a deliberate choice. That improves selection quality and helps separate rough input from durable material.

## What to remember

- Capture often fails because the user is already busy when the information appears.
- A small amount of friction can be a quality control mechanism.
- Do not optimize only for storage or generation speed.
- Use near-zero-friction capture for live tasks, but bounded friction for curated corpora.
- Better structure after the fact does not fix a bottleneck at the moment of capture.

## Consensus

- Manual capture is fragile when information arrives during live work such as meetings, chats, or calls. People often skip it because the capture action competes with the task in front of them.
- A small amount of friction at the intake boundary can improve selectivity. It forces a keep-or-skip decision and can raise the signal-to-noise ratio of what gets stored.
- Friction is most useful when the stored material is meant to be curated or reread later, not when the goal is exhaustive logging or fastest possible intake.
- For AI systems, the main design issue is not just storage or retrieval quality. It is whether capture happens without forcing a context switch away from the live task.

## Tensions / open questions

- Friction can improve quality, but too much friction slows legitimate capture and increases abandonment.
- Low-friction generation can make output easier to create, but also weaker, less owned, and more likely to become unfinished work.
- The right design depends on whether the workflow values intake speed or curation quality more. The sources do not give a universal answer.

## Practical takeaway

Design capture to be nearly invisible when speed matters, but add a small review or commitment step when the content is meant to be curated, reread, or shipped.
