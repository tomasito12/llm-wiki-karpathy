# What really matters when evaluating AI Agents for customer service?

This piece says a good AI agent evaluation is not just about accuracy scores. You also need to test how it behaves in real conversations, with messy inputs, handoffs to humans, and situations where it does not know the answer. Just as important, you should judge whether the experience feels natural and on-brand, not robotic. The article adds a third check: can the team keep improving the agent after launch through fast feedback and easy iteration? In plain terms, the question is not only “does it work?” but “can we keep making it better without getting stuck?”

## Key insights

- A proof of concept that only tests benchmark accuracy can miss failures that show up in real customer conversations.
- Evaluation should include multi-turn context, typos, incomplete questions, edge cases, rephrasings, multiple knowledge sources, and multilingual inputs.
- Resolution rate is insufficient on its own because it says little about trust, tone, or whether the conversation feels good to the customer.
- A strong handoff to a human is part of the product experience, not a separate operational detail.
- Long-term value depends on the feedback loop, iteration speed, and vendor responsiveness after launch, not just initial performance.

## Why it matters

The article is useful because it compresses a practical evaluation checklist into three durable questions: does the agent handle real-world messiness, does it feel acceptable to customers, and can the system improve after deployment. That is a better lens than over-weighting curated benchmarks, because the source explicitly argues that high scores can hide failures in context handling, rephrasing, handoff behavior, and tone. It also highlights that an AI agent is judged as a conversation partner, not just as an answer engine, which makes trust and brand fit operational criteria rather than soft preferences. The emphasis on feedback loops and iteration speed is especially valuable for teams that expect to tune behavior after launch, because it frames post-launch operations as part of evaluation. The vendor-partnership section is practical too: support quality, roadmap responsiveness, and transparency become part of the buying decision, not just procurement noise. The article is thinner on hard measurement methodology, so its value is more in shaping review criteria than in prescribing a formal scoring system. As of 2026-05-22, the guidance is actionable for teams comparing customer-service agents, but it should be treated as a qualitative framework rather than evidence of which vendors perform best.

## Limitations / open questions

The piece gives a strong checklist but no concrete scoring rubric, weighting scheme, or acceptance thresholds, so different teams could still evaluate the same agent differently. It also does not address cost, latency, security, privacy, or compliance, which can matter as much as conversation quality in deployment decisions. The advice to test real-world messiness is sound, but the article does not explain how large or representative the test set should be. The vendor-partnership criterion is important, but it is hard to operationalize without clearer evidence about how to compare vendors on roadmap influence or post-launch support. The article assumes teams have access to real customer interactions or representative testers, which may not be true in every POC.

## Contradictions / unverified claims

The strongest claim is that performance alone is insufficient, which is plausible, but the article does not show empirical proof that the proposed additional criteria predict long-term success better than benchmark performance. The vendor discussion leans on relationship quality and transparency, which are useful but somewhat subjective and may be hard to compare consistently across suppliers. The argument also risks overstating the separability of accuracy, experience, and iteration: in practice, they can interact tightly, and a single POC may not isolate them cleanly. Still, the skepticism is modest because the article is framed as evaluation advice, not as a scientific claim.
