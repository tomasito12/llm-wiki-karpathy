# I Tried Every “Second Brain” App. The Concept Is the Problem, Not the Tools.

This article is about why personal knowledge apps often fail in real work. The author tried six popular tools and found that the problem was not which app had the best templates or graphs. The real issue was that all of them still require you to stop what you are doing and write things down. That is hard during meetings, Slack chats, and calls, which is when important details usually appear. Apple Notes worked better because it was simple and fast. The article points to passive-capture tools, like Soda, as the more interesting idea as of 2026-04-01.

## Key insights

- A second-brain system fails if it optimizes organization but still depends on manual capture during active work.
- High-friction setup can replace note-taking with system-building, especially in apps like Notion and Obsidian.
- A knowledge tool can be structurally sophisticated and still lose to Apple Notes if it slows down capture at the moment of need.
- Mem’s promise of AI-assisted retrieval is not enough if the user cannot trust what gets stored or resurfaced.
- The most durable alternative proposed is passive capture: software that observes work context and records useful details without interrupting the user.

## Why it matters

The piece is useful because it isolates a practical failure mode in personal knowledge tooling: the bottleneck is not whether notes are linked, tagged, or visualized, but whether capture happens at the moment information arrives. That distinction matters for product design, because the article’s comparisons show that a strong editor, a graph view, or an object model does not solve the basic attention problem. The author’s tests also highlight a common trap for productivity software: setup richness can produce a feeling of progress without improving actual retention of useful facts. The Mem example adds a second constraint: AI-assisted retrieval is only valuable if the user can trust what was captured and surface results are consistent enough to rely on. The article is narrow and anecdotal, so it is better read as a product-design critique than as evidence about all knowledge workers. Its strongest reusable lesson is that durable note-taking systems should minimize interruption and reduce cognitive switching costs, not just improve post hoc organization. As of 2026-04-01, the argument is actionable as a design heuristic and worth monitoring, but the claim that passive capture is better remains unproven in the article itself. For meetings and adjacent workflow tools, the closing implication is relevant: if a system cannot capture details during calls, Slack threads, or client conversations without manual interruption, it will likely be abandoned.

## Limitations / open questions

The evidence is a single author’s experience across a small set of consumer apps, with no controlled benchmark, no retention metrics, and no comparison against a broader set of workflows. The article does not measure actual recall quality, time saved, or error rates, so the conclusion rests on perceived friction and abandonment. Soda is mentioned only as an intriguing premise; it is in private beta, so there is no evaluation of whether passive capture works, what it misses, or how it behaves under real privacy constraints. The article also does not address whether different users, teams, or note-taking habits would change the outcome. Economics are only lightly covered through rough subscription prices, without analysis of total cost or switching cost.

## Contradictions / unverified claims

The article’s central claim is persuasive but still mostly based on frustration, not comparative evidence. It may be true that these apps require too much manual capture for this author’s workflow, but that does not prove the concept fails broadly. The praise for Apple Notes is pragmatic, yet it may reflect low ambition rather than a universally better system. The Soda idea is interesting, but the article itself acknowledges unknown privacy implications and does not show that passive capture is more accurate or trustworthy than active capture. The strongest skepticism is that a system that remembers context automatically can trade one burden for another: surveillance, false positives, or overcollection.
