# The Sequence Opinion #872: The Cake Is a Battlefield: Who Really Controls the AI Stack

This piece looks at the AI industry stack as a business map, not just a technical one. The author uses Jensen Huang’s five-layer diagram to argue that each layer is a place where companies fight for control and profit. The key idea is that owning one layer is not enough if the layer below it becomes cheap and replaceable. What matters most is owning the scarce bottleneck and the connection right next to it. It is a short but useful lens for thinking about where power sits in the AI stack as of 2026-06-04.

## Key insights

- The five-layer AI stack can be read as a set of separate margin pools, not just a mutually reinforcing system.
- Strategic control comes from owning the scarce layer and the seam adjacent to it.
- A company that owns only a layer without control of its neighbors risks having that layer commoditized.
- The article frames the AI stack as a vertical competition over integration, not a static architecture diagram.

## Why it matters

The piece is useful because it compresses a common AI industry diagram into a sharper strategic question: where is the bottleneck, and who controls the interface around it? For AI engineers and product builders, that is a reminder that architectural choices have business consequences, especially when a dependency sits one layer below your product. The article’s main value is conceptual rather than empirical: it gives a vocabulary for thinking about capture, commoditization, and integration in the AI stack without pretending the diagram itself proves any market outcome. Its weakness is that it provides no data, case studies, or concrete examples of specific companies winning by owning a seam rather than a layer. That makes the argument easy to reuse as a mental model but hard to operationalize without separate evidence. As of 2026-06-04, it is best treated as a durable framing tool, but only as a strategic lens, not as a validated market thesis.

## Limitations / open questions

The article offers no empirical evidence for the claim that the AI stack is best understood as competing margin pools. It does not define which layers are actually scarce, how scarcity should be measured, or when a seam is defensible versus commoditized. There is no discussion of counterexamples where specialization outperforms vertical integration. The piece also leaves open how this framing applies across different segments of AI infrastructure, models, and applications, or how quickly control can shift as prices fall or standards emerge.

## Contradictions / unverified claims

The argument is rhetorically strong but lightly evidenced. Jensen Huang’s diagram may be both a real systems view and a business framing for Nvidia’s position, so the article’s reinterpretation could overcorrect by treating harmony as mostly marketing. It also risks flattening important differences between layers: some layers may be more interdependent than the article implies, and ownership of a seam does not automatically translate into durable control. The source itself provides no proof that fusion is always better than specialization.
