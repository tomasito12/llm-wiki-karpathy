# How frontier enterprises are building an AI advantage

This article is about how some firms are getting more value from enterprise AI than others. OpenAI says the difference is no longer just who has access to tools, but who uses them for deeper, more complex work. It introduces B2B Signals, a way to track how AI is being used inside companies. The main idea is that frontier firms ask AI to do more of the work, not just answer questions. The article also highlights agent-style tools and shows a couple of customer examples. The takeaway is that as of May 6, 2026, the useful question is how deeply AI is embedded in workflows, not just how many people have seats.

## Key insights

- OpenAI’s main metric is depth of use, proxied by tokens generated per worker, not just message count.
- The article claims message volume explains only 36% of the frontier-vs-typical gap, so richer prompts and outputs matter more than raw activity.
- Codex is presented as the clearest frontier marker, with frontier firms sending 16x as many messages per worker as typical firms.
- The article treats agentic tools as a maturity step because they can work across files, codebases, and longer tasks.
- OpenAI’s recommended operating model is managerial, not just technical: measure depth, enable production governance, and scale successful frontier teams.

## Why it matters

The piece is useful because it clarifies the lens OpenAI wants enterprise buyers to use when judging AI adoption: depth of workflow integration, not seat counts or generic usage. Its strongest claim is that the gap between leading and typical firms is increasingly tied to how much substantive work AI is asked to do per worker, which is a more operationally meaningful framing than simple adoption metrics. That makes the report relevant for teams trying to decide whether their AI program is producing real leverage or only light experimentation. It also highlights specific classes of tools—especially coding, deep research, and agentic workflows—that OpenAI believes are associated with frontier behavior as of May 6, 2026. The Cisco and Travelers examples provide some concrete color, but they are vendor-selected case studies rather than independent benchmarks, so they are better read as illustrations than proof. The governance and enablement advice is practical, but it is also fairly generic and not deeply specified in the article. For conversational AI, chatbots, and service automation, the most relevant signal is the move from chat-based assistance toward delegated work inside business systems, including claim intake and similar back-office flows. Actionable as of May 6, 2026, but best treated as directional vendor guidance rather than a neutral market measurement.

## Limitations / open questions

The evidence is self-reported and comes from OpenAI’s own aggregated enterprise usage data, so the framing, metrics, and interpretation are not independently verified in the article. Tokens generated are only a proxy for intelligence demanded, and the piece explicitly notes that they are not a direct measure of business value. The report does not define the full methodology for classifying frontier firms, nor does it show whether higher usage causes better outcomes or merely correlates with stronger organizations. The customer examples are selective and lack comparable baselines, cost data, failure modes, or implementation details. Security, privacy, change management, and evaluation methodology for delegated agent workflows are mentioned only at a high level, not worked through in depth. The article also does not quantify ROI across functions or industries beyond a few illustrative claims.

## Contradictions / unverified claims

The article’s main tension is that it presents a precise-sounding usage hierarchy while also acknowledging that tokens are only a proxy and message volume explains only part of the gap. That leaves room for over-reading intensity metrics as business value. The frontier narrative is also vendor-friendly: it can imply inevitability or maturity without showing whether the same practices generalize across firms with different constraints. The governance and enablement advice is sensible, but it is broad enough that it risks reading like standard enterprise AI messaging unless paired with specific operating changes. The case studies are promising but promotional, so they should not be treated as independent evidence of industry-wide impact.
