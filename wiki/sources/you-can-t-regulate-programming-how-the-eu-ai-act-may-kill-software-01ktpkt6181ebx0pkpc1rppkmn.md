# You Can’t Regulate Programming: How the EU AI Act May Kill Software

This article says the EU AI Act is not really about a few special AI models; it may sweep in a lot of ordinary software because of how “AI system” is defined. The author uses the law’s own language to argue that terms like autonomy and adaptiveness are so broad that many programs could qualify. He then shows how this creates heavy paperwork for high-risk and general-purpose models, plus labels and watermark rules for generated content. The main point is not a technical breakdown, but a warning that the law may be vague and hard to enforce. As of 2026-05-18, the piece treats the Act as a real compliance burden, but its strongest claims are rhetorical and should be read skeptically.

## Key insights

- The article’s strongest operational point is that Article 3’s definition of an AI system is broad enough, in the author’s reading, to create ambiguity for ordinary software.
- The piece highlights that compliance burden is driven not only by prohibited uses, but also by documentation, logging, human oversight, and registration duties for high-risk systems.
- For general-purpose models, the author emphasizes that training-scale thresholds, public training summaries, and copyright-policy disclosures can trigger substantial paperwork.
- The transparency section suggests that labels and watermarks may become easy-to-game formalities if companies rely on editorial or assistive-function exceptions.
- The article’s practical warning is uncertainty: the compliance boundary is presented as hard to predict, which increases risk for developers and distributors even when the underlying product is not obviously “AI.”

## Why it matters

The article is relevant because it compresses a real compliance question into one argument: how broad regulatory definitions can affect software teams even when they are not building frontier models. Its value is mainly in surfacing the law’s named categories and obligations—prohibited practices, high-risk systems, GPAI rules, and transparency duties—so practitioners can see where paperwork and product-review overhead may appear. The text is especially useful as a reminder that legal and platform compliance often get operationalized through checklists, store policies, and documentation workflows rather than through direct technical enforcement. That said, the piece is not a neutral legal analysis; it is an advocacy essay that treats the broadest reading of the Act as the likely outcome. The practical takeaway is therefore limited to risk awareness, not definitive legal interpretation. As of 2026-05-18, it is worth monitoring if you ship software into the EU, but not safe to use as a sole basis for compliance planning.

## Limitations / open questions

The article does not provide a balanced legal interpretation or case law; it relies on selected quotations from the Act and Commission guidance, then extrapolates from them. It does not show empirical evidence that ordinary software will in fact be classified as AI systems at scale, nor does it document how regulators have applied the definitions in practice. Several claims about enforcement, store takedowns, and prosecution costs are asserted without supporting evidence. The article also leaves open how exceptions, national implementation, and sector-specific guidance will narrow the apparent breadth of the rules. For practitioners, the biggest unresolved question is where the actual enforcement boundary lands for specific products and workflows.

## Contradictions / unverified claims

The article’s rhetoric often exceeds its evidence. It repeatedly treats broad definitional language as if it necessarily means universal coverage, but that is an interpretation, not a demonstrated legal outcome. Claims that violent games, ordinary services, or random software are likely to be treated as AI systems are speculative and framed for effect. The post also assumes that regulators and platforms will apply the law in the most aggressive possible way, while giving little weight to narrower readings, exceptions, or selective enforcement. Its critique of labels and watermarking may be partly fair, but the GDP-loss and deplatforming predictions are not substantiated in the text.
