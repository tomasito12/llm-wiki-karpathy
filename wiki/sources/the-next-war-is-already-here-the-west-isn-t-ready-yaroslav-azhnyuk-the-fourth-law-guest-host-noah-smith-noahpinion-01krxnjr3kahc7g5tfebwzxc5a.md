# The Next War Is Already Here. The West Isn't Ready. — Yaroslav Azhnyuk, The Fourth Law & Guest Host Noah Smith, Noahpinion

This is a conversation about how drones changed the war in Ukraine. The core idea is simple: small cheap drones, especially FPV drones, now do much of the killing on the front line, and adding AI makes them harder to jam and easier to use. The guest explains a ladder of drone autonomy, from last-mile target locking to fully autonomous missions. He also argues that the real bottlenecks are not just software, but manufacturing, sensors, fiber optic links, and supply chains. The main takeaway is that defense is now a mass-production and systems-integration problem, not just a weapons-design problem.

## Key insights

- Azhnyuk’s five-level autonomy ladder is operationally useful because it separates last-mile target lock, bombing, target detection, navigation, and takeoff/landing rather than treating “autonomy” as one blob.
- He frames FPV drones as the main frontline kill mechanism in Ukraine, claiming they account for 70–80% of frontline casualties and that terminal guidance alone raised one brigade’s mission success rate from 20% to 71%.
- Fiber optic control solves radio-jamming and radio-horizon problems, but adds weight and cost; as of the episode’s publication date, it is a tradeoff rather than a universal upgrade.
- The transcript treats drone warfare as an industrial stack problem: thermal cameras, motors, semiconductors, rare-earth refining, and mass manufacturing matter as much as computer vision.
- The strongest strategic claim is not about a single weapon but about scale: if an adversary can deploy autonomous drones at much higher volume and lower cost, the balance of conventional warfare changes fast.

## Why it matters

The piece is useful because it compresses a battlefield operator’s view of drone warfare into concrete engineering categories: autonomy, communications, sensors, manufacturing, and command structure. For AI practitioners, the most durable insight is that autonomy is not a single model feature; it is a layered capability that behaves differently across terminal guidance, navigation, target detection, and deployment scale. The guest’s taxonomy of five autonomy levels and eight battlefield dimensions gives a reusable way to reason about where machine learning helps and where classical systems still matter. The article also makes a strong cost-to-effect argument: cheap FPV drones, especially when mass-produced, can outperform far more expensive traditional systems in some land-war scenarios, while countermeasures must be judged economically as well as technically. Its supply-chain discussion is valuable because it ties autonomy to hardware chokepoints like thermal cameras, motors, fiber optic cable, and rare-earth processing rather than treating the problem as software-only. The most actionable reading as of 2026-05-18 is to treat defense AI as an integration-and-scale problem with fast iteration loops, not a lab demo problem. For service automation and voice or meeting-style workflow lessons, the article offers little directly; its value is mainly in how it frames autonomy, human-in-the-loop decisions, and reliability under adversarial conditions.

## Limitations / open questions

Many of the quantitative claims are asserted in conversation without independent verification in the transcript, including casualty shares, mission success improvements, and production comparisons. The autonomy taxonomy is useful, but the boundaries between levels are speaker-defined rather than standardized. Several strategic claims depend on hypothetical futures, such as China projecting drone power at extreme scale or autonomous systems becoming dominant in five to ten years, and the transcript does not test those assumptions empirically. The discussion gives limited detail on evaluation methods, false-positive rates, robustness under distribution shift, or how autonomous target detection is audited in practice. Economics are emphasized, but the transcript does not provide a full cost model for mass production, countermeasures, or supply-chain replacement outside Chinese sourcing.

## Contradictions / unverified claims

Some claims are intentionally provocative and should be read as argument, not settled fact. The idea that it will be immoral to use weapons without AI by five to ten years is speculative and not established by evidence in the transcript. Statements that the West lacks autonomy, mass manufacturing, components, and rare earths are broad and need separate sourcing before they can be generalized. The China-versus-West comparison is especially speculative because the transcript itself says Chinese drones in action have not been widely observed. The interview also mixes battlefield reality with rhetorical analogies like iPhones, StarCraft, and Skynet, which are helpful for intuition but can overstate linearity and inevitability.
