# The old world of tech is dying and the new cannot be born

This essay says the tech world was built on US power, and that power is weakening. The author uses examples like the DMCA case against Dmitry Sklyarov to explain how law, culture, and technology became tightly linked. The basic idea is that software companies did not just make tools; they learned to control markets, data, and customers because the global system let them do it. The article argues that this setup is breaking down because US influence, trade stability, and the petrodollar order are fraying. That matters because many software and AI business models assume the old rules still apply. The author’s warning is that those assumptions may be historical artifacts, not durable truths.

## Key insights

- The article treats technopoly as a legitimacy filter: only tech-mediated ideas count, which helps explain why copyright, regulation, and product design often favor incumbents.
- The Sklyarov/DMCA example is used to show how a legal case can enforce a broader technological ideology, not just protect one vendor’s product.
- The author argues that many software companies shifted from selling useful tools to controlling customers, industries, and distribution through subscriptions and platform power.
- Generative AI is framed as an extension of that control strategy: reduce tool-making, replace client industries, and keep the intermediary layer owned by the platform.
- The piece’s practical warning is that software strategy built on US-backed global stability may be historically contingent rather than structurally permanent.

## Why it matters

The article matters because it offers a systems-level explanation for why modern software and AI business models became so control-heavy: they were not just product choices, but choices made inside a US-backed global order that made control feasible. It ties software subscriptions, platform dominance, data access, and permissive cross-border regulation to political power rather than to pure technical merit. For an AI engineer, the useful takeaway is not the geopolitical forecasting itself, but the reminder that deployment, licensing, copyright, and market structure are not neutral constraints; they shape what kinds of systems can be built and sold. The piece is strongest when it shows how legal and institutional arrangements can make one technology stack or business model feel natural while narrowing the space for alternatives. Its claims about finance, hegemony, and international crises are largely interpretive, so they should be read as a strategic thesis rather than evidence-backed forecasting. Actionable as of 2026-05-14, the article is most useful as a prompt to audit assumptions about platform control, regulatory tolerance, and dependence on stable global infrastructure. It has limited direct guidance for service automation, voice, meetings, or back-office workflows; the relevance there is indirect, through the claim that automation products inherit their operating room from political and legal conditions.

## Limitations / open questions

The article is argumentative rather than empirical. It does not provide quantitative evidence for claims about the decline of US hegemony, the scale of financialization’s economic drag, or the causal role of specific crises in reshaping the global order. The link between those macro claims and the software industry is plausible within the author’s frame, but the operational mechanism is not tested here. The piece also compresses a lot of history into a single storyline, which makes it hard to separate durable structural analysis from rhetorical flourish. It is unclear which parts of the argument would still hold if US influence declined unevenly rather than collapsing in the sweeping way the author suggests. The article does not specify what software firms should do differently beyond recognizing that the old premises may no longer apply.

## Contradictions / unverified claims

Several claims are deliberately sweeping and should be treated cautiously. The article sometimes presents geopolitical interpretation as near-inevitable causation, especially around Iran, the petrodollar, and the end of US dominance, without showing alternative explanations or countervailing evidence. The argument that regulation like GDPR is mostly theater may be too compressed; the text asserts this as a pattern but does not compare outcomes rigorously. The claim that the software industry could not exist without US empire is provocative but overstated as written, since the article does not distinguish between current scale, historical growth conditions, and possible future adaptation. Still, the internal logic is coherent, and the main skepticism is about certainty and scope rather than obvious inconsistency.
