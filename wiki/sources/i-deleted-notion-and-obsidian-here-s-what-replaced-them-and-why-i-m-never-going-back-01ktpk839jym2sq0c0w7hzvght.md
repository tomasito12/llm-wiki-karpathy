# I Deleted Notion and Obsidian. Here’s What Replaced Them — and Why I’m Never Going Back.

This piece is about swapping traditional note-taking and workspace apps for AI tools that do more of the organizing work for you. The author says Notion and Obsidian turned into projects to maintain, not places to get work done. NotebookLM is described as a way to ask questions over your saved notes instead of hunting through folders or backlinks. Gemini Canvas is presented as a simpler replacement for building documents and trackers by hand. The main idea is not that these tools are perfect, but that they reduce setup and keep the system from becoming the job.

## Key insights

- Notion’s flexibility can create a hidden maintenance burden when solo users keep expanding simple notes into full database systems.
- Obsidian’s usefulness depends heavily on remembering what to search for and on having already built a backlink structure.
- NotebookLM changes the retrieval model from keyword search to question answering over added sources, which reduces the cost of forgetting where information lives.
- Gemini Canvas is positioned as a prompt-driven substitute for hand-designed document structure, with inline revision instead of full regeneration.
- The article’s strongest practical claim is about friction reduction, not feature superiority: the winning system is the one that does not require ongoing administration.

## Why it matters

The piece is useful as a compact description of a real workflow tradeoff: some productivity tools reward system design, while others reduce the need for it. For AI practitioners building knowledge workflows, the interesting part is the shift from note organization to source-grounded retrieval and generation. The author’s NotebookLM example shows a low-friction pattern: add material once, then query it later without relying on a manually curated folder structure or backlink graph. The Gemini Canvas section adds a second pattern: generate structured working documents on demand and edit them in place instead of maintaining templates and schemas. That is a practical reminder that the value of AI assistants in personal knowledge work may come less from raw capability than from lowering setup and upkeep costs. The article is thin on evidence, so the claims should be treated as a personal workflow report rather than a general proof. Actionable as of 2026-05-12, but best read as a user-experience anecdote to test, not a settled recommendation.

## Limitations / open questions

The article provides no benchmarks, task completion comparisons, or retention/quality measures, so it does not establish that NotebookLM or Gemini are better in general. It also does not discuss privacy, data governance, offline access, export portability beyond Google Docs/Sheets, or what happens when source libraries become large. The argument assumes the author’s pain point is note organization overhead, which may not apply to users who value manual control, local storage, or long-term archival structure. It is unclear how robust NotebookLM’s source synthesis is across messy notes, conflicting sources, or very large knowledge bases. The article does not compare cost, subscription tiers, or lock-in risks against the replaced tools.

## Contradictions / unverified claims

The article treats Notion and Obsidian as if their flexibility is mainly a liability, but many users choose them specifically for that control. It also assumes that AI-assisted retrieval and document generation are a cleaner replacement than manual systems without showing error rates or failure modes. The claim that these tools eliminate maintenance may be overstated, since any source-based workflow still needs curation, verification, and some structure. The piece is persuasive as personal testimony, but it does not prove that the new setup scales better or lasts longer for other workflows.
