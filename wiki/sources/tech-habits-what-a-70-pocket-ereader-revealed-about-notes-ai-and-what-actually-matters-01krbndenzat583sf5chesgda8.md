# Tech Habits: What a $70 Pocket eReader Revealed About Notes, AI, and What Actually Matters

This is a personal essay about using a cheap pocket eReader to think more clearly about notes and AI. The device matters because it is small, single-purpose, and slightly inconvenient to load, so every new item has to earn its place. That turns reading into a filter for what is worth keeping. The author likes handwritten, carefully distilled prose more than AI summaries because the choosing process makes the notes feel like theirs. The basic idea is simple: use tools that create just enough friction to protect attention and preserve judgment.

## Key insights

- A small amount of loading friction can be a feature, because it forces a deliberate decision about what belongs in a focused reading device.
- Progressive summarization works best when done by hand, because the act of selecting and compressing content is itself part of the insight.
- AI summaries can be accurate yet still feel hollow if they skip the human judgment step that makes notes personally meaningful.
- A useful split is transactional AI generation for code or other disposable outputs versus human-crafted writing for material meant to be reread.
- The author’s preferred workflow is AI-assisted capture and suggestion, followed by human selection of the final sentences that matter.

## Why it matters

The article is useful because it frames attention management as an engineering and workflow design problem, not just a productivity preference. Its core point is that when AI lowers the cost of generating text, summaries, and code, the bottleneck shifts toward deciding what is worth keeping and rereading. That is a durable observation for anyone building note systems, personal knowledge workflows, or tools that sit between raw capture and reusable prose. The piece also gives a concrete distinction between outputs you want generated transactionally and outputs you want crafted carefully, which is a helpful abstraction for AI-assisted writing tools. The strongest operational insight is the value of bounded friction: a simple device or workflow constraint can improve curation by making every item earn its place. The claim about hand-made notes preserving authenticity is plausible, but it is based on personal experience rather than comparative evaluation. As of 2026-04-20, the idea looks actionable as a workflow principle, but it should be treated as a personal practice pattern rather than evidence of a general best practice. The service-automation angle is minimal here; the closest implication is that AI can help with capture and drafting, but the author argues human judgment should remain in the loop for what gets stored, reread, or maintained.

## Limitations / open questions

The evidence is a single-person reflection, so it does not establish that the device or method improves recall, comprehension, or long-term productivity for other users. There is no benchmark, usability study, or controlled comparison between AI summaries, hand-written progressive summarization, and other note systems. The article also does not quantify the cost of the extra friction it advocates, such as time spent curating or the risk of losing useful rough captures. It is unclear how well the approach scales if a person has a much larger corpus, many collaborators, or stricter retrieval needs. The discussion of AI maintaining personal wiki pages is conceptual, not an evaluation of accuracy, maintenance burden, or failure modes.

## Contradictions / unverified claims

The essay treats AI-generated summaries as potentially hollow, but that judgment is asserted from taste and workflow experience rather than tested against different note-use cases. The claim that the human should decide what matters may be right for reflective notes, but it may be too restrictive for high-volume capture systems where speed and recall dominate. The device is presented as a meaningful filter, yet the same friction could also become an arbitrary barrier if the goal is broad archiving rather than curated rereading. The discussion of a fully LLM-maintained personal wiki is interesting, but the article does not show whether that model actually fails in practice or simply conflicts with the author’s preference for authored prose.
