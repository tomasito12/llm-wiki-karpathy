# the solution might be cancelling my AI subscription

This piece is about why AI can make people busier without making their work better. The author says AI helped them build lots of things, but many of those projects were unused, unfinished, or not worth keeping. Their main point is that when a tool makes it too easy to create output, it can also make it too easy to lose focus. The article uses one failed experiment, turning speech into blog posts, to show that removing effort also removed care and quality. It argues that useful work still needs friction, commitment, and a clear outcome. As of 2026-05-31, the practical takeaway is to be careful with AI usage when attention and maintainability matter.

## Key insights

- Low-friction AI can generate many artifacts, but the author argues that ease of creation often comes at the cost of commitment and maintenance.
- The strongest complaint is not model quality but attention capture: the tooling encourages more sessions, more tokens, and more shallow work.
- A fast tool can still be harmful if it keeps producing outputs that are not tied to a real problem or a maintained workflow.
- The article’s failed speech-to-blog pipeline is a concrete example of why removing effort can also remove quality and meaning.
- The piece suggests that restricting usage can be a rational way to protect focus when the work depends on judgment and sustained attention.

## Why it matters

The article is useful because it captures a practical failure mode of AI-assisted development that is easy to miss when demos look impressive: generation speed can outpace project selection, maintenance capacity, and user commitment. The author grounds this in first-person experience with Claude and Codex, plus a long list of AI-built projects that ranged from genuinely useful to abandoned or forgotten, which makes the critique more concrete than a generic anti-AI take. The most durable insight is that tooling design and vendor incentives matter; if every interface nudges toward more prompts, more follow-up, and more output, it becomes easier to accumulate unfinished work than to ship durable value. The article also usefully distinguishes between “can build it” and “should keep it,” which is a real judgment problem for AI practitioners building internal tools or side projects. The Cal Newport reference adds a familiar frame: visible activity can substitute for meaningful progress. The evidence is still anecdotal and self-reported, so the piece is more diagnostic than empirical. As of 2026-05-31, the takeaway is to treat AI as something to constrain deliberately when the goal is quality, not volume. The service-automation implication is indirect rather than central: if AI is used in workflow-heavy settings, the same attention and pseudo-productivity risks can show up in support, meetings, or back-office pipelines, so friction and outcome checks matter there too.

## Limitations / open questions

The evidence is mostly one person’s experience, so it does not establish how common these effects are across teams or roles. The article does not provide measurements of time saved, output quality, retention, or maintenance cost, so the claims about wasted effort remain qualitative. It also does not separate model capability from workflow design very cleanly: some failures may come from poor project selection rather than AI itself. The piece argues for curtailing use, but it does not specify operational guardrails, review thresholds, or team-level policies that would preserve useful AI while limiting overuse. The speech-to-blog example shows one bad pipeline, but it does not prove that all voice-to-text or capture workflows are low value; value likely depends on the task and constraints.

## Contradictions / unverified claims

The article’s strongest claims are subjective and may overgeneralize from a few personal examples. Saying AI is “horrific” for attention is rhetorically vivid, but the text does not compare against alternatives or quantify the effect. The complaint that vendors optimize for more tokens and more interaction is plausible, yet the article offers only interface observation rather than systematic evidence. The invocation of widespread suffering from similar effects in adult friends is anecdotal and not enough to support a broad claim. The conclusion that the answer is curtailing use may be right for the author, but the piece does not show where the line should be between disciplined use and needless restriction.
