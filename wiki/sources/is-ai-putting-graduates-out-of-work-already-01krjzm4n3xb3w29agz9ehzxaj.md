# Is AI putting graduates out of work already?

This article asks whether AI is already making it harder for new graduates to get hired. The short answer is: maybe, especially for computer science and other coding-heavy majors. The Economist says graduate outcomes in the most AI-exposed fields have fallen more sharply since ChatGPT launched in 2022. But the evidence is not one-sided, because some studies say the decline started before ChatGPT and affects senior workers too. The practical takeaway is that the job market for entry-level technical talent looks weaker in May 2026, even if the exact role of AI is still debated.

## Key insights

- The strongest evidence in the article is not a single study but a comparison of graduate outcomes across fields with different AI exposure.
- Computer science and related majors appear more vulnerable than less-exposed subjects such as education, philosophy, and civil engineering.
- The article’s own analysis finds the steepest post-2022 employment drop in the most AI-exposed quintile of majors.
- Enrollment shifts suggest students are already reacting to perceived labor-market risk, not just reading headlines.
- The main uncertainty is causality: the decline may reflect AI, but some cited studies say the trend began before ChatGPT and is not confined to junior workers.

## Why it matters

For AI engineering readers, the useful point is that the article ties model adoption to labor-market effects at the entry level, not just to productivity or user experience. It suggests that when AI can absorb routine coding or narrow technical tasks, the first visible pressure may land on new graduates whose jobs are easiest to standardize and compare. The piece is also useful methodologically because it relies on field-level exposure analysis and graduate survey data rather than anecdotes, which makes the signal more durable than a single employer story. At the same time, the article is careful enough to show that the evidence is mixed: some cited work finds declines predating ChatGPT, so AI may be amplifying a preexisting slowdown rather than causing it from scratch. For product builders, this is a reminder that AI can reshape the composition of junior work even before it clearly eliminates whole roles. The article does not discuss customer support, contact centers, voicebots, or back-office automation in depth, so those implications are not central here. Actionable as of May 13, 2026, but the causal story should be treated as provisional rather than settled.

## Limitations / open questions

The article gives suggestive but not definitive causal evidence. Its own analysis compares outcomes across fields with different AI exposure, but that design cannot fully separate AI effects from other changes affecting tech hiring, macro conditions, or curriculum shifts. The external studies it cites also disagree on whether young workers are uniquely affected. The graduate survey data may miss job quality, part-time work, or underemployment beyond the full-time employment metric. It also does not quantify how much of the decline is directly due to models replacing tasks versus firms freezing hiring or changing job descriptions.

## Contradictions / unverified claims

The article pushes back on the simple claim that AI is already destroying graduate jobs, and the cited research does not fully support that headline. One tension is that the observed decline in AI-exposed occupations may have started before ChatGPT, which weakens any clean attribution to generative AI. Another is that the evidence comes from employment outcomes and postings, not direct observation of AI substituting for specific workers. The piece is strongest as a warning signal about entry-level technical hiring, not as proof of a universal AI-driven labor-market collapse.
