# McDonald’s Abandons Drive Through AI for Order Taking

McDonald’s had been testing voice technology to take orders at drive-thrus, but this article says that test with IBM will end. The company is not necessarily giving up on voice ordering altogether. Instead, it seems to be stepping back from one version of the technology and looking for other options. The article says there were problems with accents, dialects, and incorrect orders. It also suggests that newer generative artificial intelligence systems may understand spoken language better than older systems. Even so, the article says there are still hard problems like noisy restaurant environments and complicated menu language. The main message is that the idea is still alive, but the first attempt did not fully solve the real-world problems. As of 2024-06-18, this looks like a case to watch rather than a finished success story.

## Key insights

- Ending one pilot does not prove the use case is dead; it can also mean the first architecture was not robust enough.
- Accent, dialect, and noise handling remain first-order constraints in drive-thru speech systems, not edge cases.
- Generative AI is presented as potentially better than older natural-language-understanding approaches for spoken order capture.
- Restaurant labor shortages are part of the business case, but the source gives no operational metrics, so the economic upside remains qualitative here.

## Why it matters

McDonald’s ending this IBM test is a useful reminder that conversational AI in the physical world is judged by failure modes, not demos. The source points to a concrete set of hard constraints: accents, dialects, environmental noise, and complex quick-service restaurant vocabularies. That combination makes order-taking a much tougher problem than generic voice dictation, because small recognition errors become customer-facing transaction errors. The article also suggests that the model choice matters: generative AI may handle natural language better than the older NLU-based approach that struggled here, but the text does not provide evidence that generative AI has already solved the full problem. For practitioners, the durable lesson is to treat voice order-taking as a systems problem involving speech, language understanding, and operational context, not just a model swap. As of 2024-06-18, the evidence supports cautious monitoring of this use case rather than assuming either failure or breakthrough. For service automation, the closing implication is specific: drive-thru and other front-line voice workflows still need noise tolerance, human-in-the-loop fallback, and error handling before they can be trusted at scale.

## Limitations / open questions

The article relies on a CNBC report and a franchisee memo rather than operational metrics, so there is no hard data on accuracy, latency, cost, or customer satisfaction. It is unclear how much of the failure was due to the IBM system itself versus deployment design, menu complexity, or site-specific conditions. The piece also does not say whether McDonald’s will replace this test with another vendor, another model class, or a different rollout design. The claim that generative AI handles accents and dialects better is plausible but unsupported here by measurements.

## Contradictions / unverified claims

The article hints that the objective may continue even if the IBM approach ends, so treating this as a categorical rejection of voice ordering would overread the evidence. The generative-AI angle is suggestive but not demonstrated with comparative results. More broadly, the source is opinionated and narrative-driven; it offers useful operational clues, but not a full case study or benchmark.
