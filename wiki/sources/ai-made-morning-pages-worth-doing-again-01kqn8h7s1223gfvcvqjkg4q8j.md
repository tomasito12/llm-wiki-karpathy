# AI made morning pages worth doing again.

This piece is about using AI to make a classic journaling habit easier to keep. The author says morning pages worked better once they stopped being only a brain dump and became a way to feed tasks and questions into an AI assistant. The key move is simple: write for 15 minutes, ask the AI to handle interruptions, then have it extract to-dos from the text. That makes the habit feel more useful and easier to repeat. The article also notes a tension: AI can help writing, but some studies suggest it can also make people rely on it too much.

## Key insights

- Time-boxing morning pages to 15 minutes makes the habit more predictable and easier to sustain than an open-ended page target.
- Inline AI matters because it lets the writer delegate questions without opening a new tab and breaking flow.
- Turning morning pages into a task list gives the practice an immediate payoff, which the author says is what changed adherence.
- The author treats AI as a support for offloading thoughts and small lookups, not as a replacement for the writing practice itself.
- The article’s own evidence is personal and anecdotal, so the strongest claim is about one workable workflow, not a general rule.

## Why it matters

The piece is useful because it shows a concrete, low-complexity way to embed AI into a writing habit without turning the habit into a heavy automation project. The main engineering lesson is not about model quality but about workflow design: reduce friction, keep the user in flow, and make the output immediately useful. The author’s setup combines three durable ideas that are broadly reusable in product design: time boxing, inline assistance, and post-hoc structuring of freeform text into actionable items. That makes the article more interesting than a generic “AI helps writing” claim, because it specifies the mechanism that improved adherence. The evidence is still narrow, since the benefits come from one person’s experience and not from controlled testing. The external studies mentioned about reduced brain activity and worse performance when AI is removed add a useful caution, but they are not integrated into a stronger evaluation framework here. The article is therefore best read as a practical workflow anecdote, not as proof that AI improves journaling or cognition. As of 2026-04-29, the setup looks actionable as a lightweight writing aid, but its durability is still unproven beyond the author’s own use.

## Limitations / open questions

The evidence base is thin: this is a first-person account, so it does not show whether the workflow generalizes beyond one writer. The article does not define how often the AI-generated todo list is accurate, whether it misses important items, or whether it creates false tasks from reflective writing. It also does not address privacy or retention concerns for journaling data sent to an AI assistant. The mention of MIT and Wharton findings is interesting, but the article does not provide the study details needed to judge how comparable those contexts are to this workflow. It is also unclear whether the productivity gain comes from AI itself or from the simpler time-boxing and task extraction structure.

## Contradictions / unverified claims

The article leans on a tension it does not fully resolve: AI is presented as both a cognitive aid and something that can make users dumber. The claim that “AI made morning pages worth doing again” may be more about motivation and habit design than about AI’s unique intelligence. The author also suggests the practice preserves the core benefits of morning pages, but that is hard to verify without a stronger baseline or comparison. The company-specific mention of Medium’s TK adds product context, but the article is still primarily a personal workflow endorsement rather than evidence of a robust new writing method.
