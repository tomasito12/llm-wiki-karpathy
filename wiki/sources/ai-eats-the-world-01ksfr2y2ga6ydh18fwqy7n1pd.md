# AI Eats The World

This piece says AI is not just about better models. It is also about a huge wave of spending on chips, data centers, and engineering capacity. The author thinks that matters because the expensive part may not end up being where the profits are. A lot of the article asks a simple question: if AI makes some tasks much cheaper, what products, jobs, or workflows get broken apart? The basic warning is that usage is growing fast, but many people are still experimenting rather than relying on AI every day. As of May 2026, the useful mindset is to watch the economics and the bottlenecks, not just the benchmark charts.

## Key insights

- The article frames AI as a capital deployment problem as much as a model-quality problem: spending, construction, and supply constraints are central to the argument.
- Evans suggests frontier LLMs may behave like commoditized infrastructure for many general-use cases, with limited evidence of network effects in the text.
- The piece distinguishes wide experimentation from deep adoption; many users and workplaces appear to be trying AI without making it a daily essential.
- A recurring strategic question is whether AI unbundles whole software products or only automates narrow tasks inside existing workflows.
- The most durable opportunity may sit above the model layer in tooling, workflows, data, and distribution, not necessarily in the base model itself.

## Why it matters

The article is useful because it forces AI builders to think in economic layers, not just capability layers. Its strongest claim is that the immediate story in May 2026 is massive capex: hyperscalers, labs, and chip vendors are pouring money into compute, and that spending is running into manufacturing and construction bottlenecks. That makes infrastructure planning, financing structure, and supply-chain capacity first-class product concerns, not back-office details. The piece also gives a sober frame for model strategy: if frontier models are already similar on many general tasks and do not show obvious network effects, then owning the model may not be the same as owning the market. For product teams, the more durable question is which tasks can be made cheap enough to unbundle software categories or create new workflows around probabilistic systems. The article is especially useful in pushing readers to separate task automation from job redesign and from new question generation. It is less strong as evidence for any single market forecast, because much of the case is interpretive and stitched together from charts, quotes, and examples rather than a controlled study. As of May 2026, the right use is as a strategic lens to monitor, not as a settled operating manual.

## Limitations / open questions

The piece is thesis-driven and relies heavily on selective charts, executive quotes, and qualitative comparisons rather than a single rigorous causal dataset. Several claims are explicitly provisional, including the idea that LLMs are commodities and that network effects are weak. It does not resolve where durable profits will accrue if base models commoditize, or how long the current supply-demand imbalance can sustain premium pricing. The article also leaves open how to measure real productivity gains versus experimentation, and how to distinguish shallow usage from workflows that become economically central. Its platform-shift analogies are suggestive, but they do not by themselves predict which specific AI products or business models will win.

## Contradictions / unverified claims

The argument is persuasive as a framing device, but some of the strongest conclusions are still underdetermined by the evidence shown. Treating leading models as commodities may be premature if model differentiation, distribution, or agentic tooling becomes more important than the charted benchmark scores imply. The comparison to mobile networks and other platform shifts is helpful, but analogies can flatten important differences in deployment speed, regulation, and enterprise integration. The article also leans on large spending numbers, yet high capex alone does not guarantee durable returns or clear value capture. The most cautious reading is that the piece is best at naming the questions that matter, not at closing them.
