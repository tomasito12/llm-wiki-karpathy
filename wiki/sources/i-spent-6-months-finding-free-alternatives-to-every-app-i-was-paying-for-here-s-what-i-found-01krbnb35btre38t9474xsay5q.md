# I Spent 6 Months Finding Free Alternatives to Every App I Was Paying For. Here’s What I Found.

This article is a personal experiment in cutting software subscriptions down to zero where possible. The author tried free tools for note-taking, design, scheduling, focus, storage, invoicing, and team communication. The basic idea is simple: many people pay for features they do not actually use. The piece argues that some free tools are good enough, and in a few cases easier to live with than the paid ones. It is interesting because it gives concrete replacements instead of vague advice. The main takeaway is that a cheaper stack can still be workable if you choose tools that cover the daily basics well.

## Key insights

- Obsidian can replace paid note apps for solo workflows when plain Markdown files and no cloud lock-in matter more than bundled collaboration.
- Photopea is presented as a browser-based Photoshop substitute that preserves enough familiar editing behavior to handle PSD work for many cases.
- Cal.com’s free hosted plan includes core scheduling features that remove the need for Calendly for simple booking flows.
- Cold Turkey’s free version is useful because system-level blocking is harder to bypass than browser-based focus tools.
- The article’s strongest operational claim is that replacing paid apps reduced workflow friction, not just monthly spending.

## Why it matters

The article is useful because it turns an abstract “use free alternatives” idea into a concrete stack with specific tradeoffs, and it does so from actual use on projects and deadlines rather than from feature-page comparison. For AI or software practitioners who manage a lot of individual tools, the durable lesson is not that every paid app is wasteful, but that many subscription costs survive by inertia while core functionality can often be met by simpler free products. The roundup also highlights a useful evaluation pattern: test whether a tool covers your highest-frequency tasks, whether its free tier has a hidden expiry or history limit, and whether the replacement removes friction rather than adding it. The article’s savings estimate and role-based guide make it operationally relevant as a budgeting and stack-audit reference, although it remains a personal anecdote rather than a measured benchmark. Several recommendations are strongest when the task is narrow and well-bounded, such as formatting, scheduling, blocking sites, or one-off file transfer. The communication tools section is especially relevant for lightweight meetings and async updates, since Google Meet, Slack, and Loom are positioned as adequate free-tier substitutes for short calls, live chat, and brief video messages. As of 2026-04-25, this is actionable as a practical checklist, but it should be treated as a user report, not evidence that free tiers broadly dominate paid software.

## Limitations / open questions

The evidence is entirely anecdotal: one person’s six-month workflow test, with no formal benchmarking, usability study, or failure-rate comparison. Several claims depend on the author’s specific use patterns, such as “solo users,” short client calls, or limited focus-block lists, so generalization is uncertain. Some tools have meaningful free-tier constraints that may matter for heavier users, including Slack’s 90-day searchable history, Loom’s video cap, WeTransfer’s size and expiry limits, and Calendar/drive platform dependencies. Security and privacy tradeoffs are mentioned only in broad terms; for example, MEGA’s zero-knowledge claim is asserted but not examined operationally. The article does not quantify switching costs, migration effort, reliability over time, or whether free plans might change pricing or limits later. The savings figure is useful as a rough estimate, but the underlying assumptions are not shown in detail.

## Contradictions / unverified claims

The piece is persuasive, but it leans on convenience and personal satisfaction more than on comparative evidence. Claims like “indistinguishable for 90% of use cases” are plausible in narrow contexts but are not supported by controlled testing here. The statement that the free alternatives are “genuinely better” in several cases is subjective and may reflect the author’s workflow rather than a broad product ranking. Some recommendations are also tied to acceptance of branding, usage caps, or reduced archives, which may be fine for individuals but not for teams with stricter operational requirements. Overall, the skepticism is not that the tools are unusable; it is that the article compresses a lot of nuanced product fit into a simple savings narrative.
