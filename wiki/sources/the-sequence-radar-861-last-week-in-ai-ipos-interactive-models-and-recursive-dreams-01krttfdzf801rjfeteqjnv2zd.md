# The Sequence Radar #861: Last Week in AI: IPOs, Interactive Models, and Recursive Dreams

This is a weekly AI news roundup centered on one idea: agency. Instead of focusing on bigger models, it spotlights systems that can interact, remember, and improve themselves. Cerebras’s IPO is used to show that AI still depends on very real hardware and power infrastructure. Thinking Machines is described as building models that stay in the loop during a conversation, not just answer once and leave. The article also covers “AI scientist” startups that automate research loops, plus several agent and safety research papers. The basic takeaway is that, as of 2026-05-17, the interesting work is shifting toward models that can do more than generate text: they can collaborate, adapt, and help build the next version of themselves.

## Key insights

- Cerebras is framed as evidence that AI infrastructure remains a capital-intensive, physical bottleneck, not just a software problem.
- Thinking Machines’ interaction models treat back-and-forth interaction as part of the model, not merely a wrapper around it.
- Recursive Superintelligence and Adaption’s AutoScientist both target automated research loops, but the roundup explicitly warns that self-improvement raises grounding and safety concerns.
- The China lab item is not just funding news; it is presented as a test of whether open-model talent can sustain a new independent lab under compute constraints and competition from Alibaba and ByteDance.
- The research list clusters around memory, agent training, and lightweight safety, suggesting the practical frontier is making agents more reliable and cheaper, not only more capable.

## Why it matters

The article matters because it compresses several concrete bets about where AI engineering effort is going as of 2026-05-17: hardware companies still need massive infrastructure, product teams are experimenting with interaction-native models, and startup labs are trying to automate parts of research itself. Cerebras’s IPO is presented as a market signal that compute infrastructure remains strategically important because every chatbot still sits on top of silicon, power, networking, and cooling. Thinking Machines’ preview suggests a different product architecture: instead of treating conversation as a UI layer, it makes listening, interrupting, yielding, and reacting part of the model’s behavior. That is useful for anyone designing agents that need to collaborate over time rather than emit one-shot answers. The “AI scientist” items are more speculative, but they are operationally relevant because they target the loop of diagnosis, experimentation, and model adaptation, which is a plausible source of leverage if the systems can stay grounded. The research roundup reinforces that the next practical gains may come from memory quality, agent scaffolding, and compact safety models rather than only from larger base models. The China lab item adds a real-world constraint check: open-model momentum only becomes durable if it can survive export controls, limited compute, and overlap with existing giants. The piece is actionable as of 2026-05-17, but several claims remain early-stage previews or fundraising reports rather than validated production evidence. For service automation, the closest implication is that interaction-native models and better memory may improve multi-turn workflows and back-office assistants, but the article does not substantiate those deployments directly.

## Limitations / open questions

Several items are launch announcements, funding reports, or editorial framings rather than independently verified long-term results. The interaction-model claim is interesting, but the article does not provide benchmarks, latency data, deployment details, or failure modes. Recursive self-improvement and AutoScientist are conceptually compelling, yet the text itself acknowledges the open question of whether capability gains can be achieved without weakening grounding, safety, diversity, or human judgment. The research summaries describe benchmark wins, but not the exact evaluation settings, robustness, or cost. The China lab story depends on reported fundraising terms and on whether a new lab can actually obtain enough compute under export constraints. The roundup does not establish which of these developments will translate into durable product advantage versus temporary excitement.

## Contradictions / unverified claims

The roundup is enthusiastic about agency and recursive improvement, but its strongest claims are still largely directional rather than demonstrated at scale. The language around models that “listen, watch, interrupt, yield, and react” is compelling, yet the article does not show whether that interaction style improves task completion enough to justify added complexity. The “AI scientist” framing is especially speculative because automated self-improvement can just as easily optimize proxies or amplify mistakes if the evaluation loop is weak. The Cerebras IPO is informative as a market event, but an IPO price move is not the same thing as proof of a better technical or product moat.
