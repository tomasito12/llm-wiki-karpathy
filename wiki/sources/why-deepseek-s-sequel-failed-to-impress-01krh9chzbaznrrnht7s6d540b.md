# Why DeepSeek’s sequel failed to impress

A Chinese artificial intelligence lab called DeepSeek made headlines a little over a year earlier because it released models that performed almost as well as top Western systems but were much cheaper to build. Its new model, v4, did not create the same excitement. The article says the new model is still strong and still cheap for users, but it may not have been nearly as cheap to train as the earlier one. It also says DeepSeek is facing tougher competition inside China from other model makers and from big internet companies that are building products on top of artificial intelligence. Those companies are focusing less on the raw model and more on apps that help people do things and move money or goods. The piece also points to more government control in China, including pressure to use local chips and a blocked acquisition. The overall message is that a clever model alone may no longer be enough to stand out. As of April 2026, the article treats DeepSeek’s latest release as important but not decisive.

## Key insights

- DeepSeek v4 is presented as strong on user price/performance, but the article argues that the training-cost breakthrough that made its earlier release famous is no longer clearly demonstrated.
- The Chinese AI market described here is crowded enough that model quality alone is no longer the only differentiator; app-layer distribution matters more in the article’s framing.
- The omission of a v4 training-cost figure is itself treated as a meaningful signal, because the earlier $6m claim was central to DeepSeek’s reputation.
- State intervention is not treated as background noise: the article says chip choices and merger controls are directly shaping AI company strategy in China.
- The article’s Anthropic comparison suggests that capability and safety governance are being discussed together, but it gives no technical evidence for DeepSeek’s own safeguards.

## Why it matters

This piece matters because it shows how quickly a breakthrough narrative can lose force when competitors catch up and the surrounding market changes. DeepSeek’s v4 still appears competitive on performance and pricing, but the article suggests that those traits are no longer enough to recreate the earlier panic. For AI teams, the practical lesson is that headline efficiency gains matter most when they are both extreme and auditable; once the source stops disclosing training cost, the market story becomes harder to sustain. The article also highlights a product-strategy point: Chinese internet firms are putting more emphasis on app layers, digital workflows, and “super apps” than on model releases alone. That makes distribution and integration part of the value proposition, not just model benchmarks. The state-intervention angle is material because chip availability and regulatory approval are shaping what gets built and shipped. For service automation, the article’s most concrete signal is that Alibaba is already using Qwen in a merchant-facing “digital workforce,” which suggests models are being pulled into operational apps rather than treated as standalone demos. As of 2026-04-28, the article reads as a useful but source-limited snapshot: actionable for strategy framing, not enough by itself to justify sweeping conclusions.

## Limitations / open questions

The article relies heavily on company tests for performance claims, and it does not provide independent benchmarks for v4. It also gives no training-cost estimate for v4, which makes the central cost-efficiency comparison less auditable than the earlier DeepSeek release. The discussion of safeguards is thin: the article notes that DeepSeek’s documents do not mention them, but it does not inspect the model or its deployment policies. It is also unclear how much of DeepSeek’s reduced splash is due to technical limits versus market saturation and state constraints. The article mentions app-layer monetization, but it does not quantify whether those products are commercially succeeding.

## Contradictions / unverified claims

There is a tension between the claim that v4 remains highly capable and cheap for customers and the article’s suggestion that the release failed to impress; the gap seems to come from the loss of a dramatic cost story rather than a collapse in quality. The comparison with Anthropic’s cautious release is suggestive but incomplete, because the article does not establish whether DeepSeek’s safeguards are actually weaker or merely undocumented. The piece also hints at a competitive landscape where model leadership matters less, but it does not provide hard evidence that app-layer products are already monetizing better than models across the board.
