# AI models are being used to predict conflict

This piece is about using AI to guess where conflict might break out or intensify. The basic idea is simple: feed models lots of clues from past violence, social data, weather, economics, and even satellite images, then look for patterns humans might miss. Some systems are already in use by think tanks, UN bodies, and defense-related groups. But the article is careful: these tools seem better at tracking violence that is already happening than at predicting a brand-new uprising. It also warns that bad data, disinformation, and the act of forecasting itself can create problems. As of 2026-05-13, the work looks useful but still constrained and uneven.

## Key insights

- Conflict forecasting systems appear strongest when they have dense historical data and are predicting continued violence rather than first onset.
- Non-traditional signals such as satellite imagery, traffic patterns, weather, and social media are being fused with standard conflict indicators.
- ACLED’s CAST and similar systems are operational enough to be used by UN agencies and foreign ministries, not just researchers.
- Forecasting models are being extended beyond violence to displacement prediction, as shown by the IOM’s Somalia estimate.
- A major risk is that forecasts may alter behavior: regimes can crack down preemptively, and vulnerable groups may become easier to target.

## Why it matters

The article is useful because it shows where AI forecasting has crossed from academic curiosity into operational use, while still exposing clear limits. RAND’s Iran example illustrates the appeal of model-based probabilities, but also how fragile such forecasts are when they lack classified inputs and human review. The broader set of systems described here share a common design: combine historical conflict records with many weak signals, then search for patterns that may precede unrest or displacement. That makes the piece relevant to anyone building prediction pipelines over sparse, noisy, politically sensitive data, especially when evaluation depends on rare events and incomplete labels. It also highlights a practical distinction between forecasting ongoing violence and predicting new conflict onset; the former is described as more dependable, the latter as unreliable. The operational lesson is that better feature coverage does not remove ambiguity about hidden triggers or manipulated public signals. For service automation, the article offers little direct guidance beyond noting that one IOM system forecasts displacement rather than chat or workflow tasks. Actionable as of 2026-05-13, but best treated as a constrained, high-uncertainty forecasting domain rather than a solved application.

## Limitations / open questions

The article repeatedly notes that good data are hard to come by, especially for new-conflict onset where the key trigger may not be observable in public indicators. Several systems are described without details on calibration, error rates, or out-of-sample performance beyond isolated examples like Ceará and Somalia. Social media signals are increasingly compromised by disinformation, which weakens a major input class. The Iran forecast was produced without classified intelligence and without human vetting, so its real-world reliability is hard to judge. There is also an unresolved governance question: if forecasts encourage repression or expose vulnerable populations, the model may increase harm even when statistically useful.

## Contradictions / unverified claims

The piece is enthusiastic about newer models, but the evidence remains uneven: some examples are specific and operational, while others are aspirational. The 20% Iran collapse estimate sounds precise, yet the article gives no benchmark showing it is better than expert judgment or alternative models. Claims about spotting patterns invisible to humans are plausible but unproven here. The article’s own experts disagree on usefulness, with some saying models are only helpful once strife has already started. That tension makes the central promise real but still unvalidated for the hardest case: predicting first onset of conflict.
