# The tech jobs bust is real. Don’t blame AI (yet)

This article asks whether AI is causing the recent slump in tech hiring. Its answer is: not yet, or at least not for the main reasons people assume. Big tech firms are cutting jobs, but the piece argues that higher interest rates, the end of pandemic-era hiring, and outsourcing explain a lot of the slowdown. It also says AI tools were too weak for much of this period to replace software engineers at scale. The interesting twist is that tech workers are still being hired outside big tech, especially in non-tech industries. So the story is less “tech jobs are vanishing” and more “tech skills are moving around the economy.”

## Key insights

- Do not date AI-driven labor displacement from ChatGPT's release alone; the article says that marker is misleading because early tools were too primitive.
- Higher interest rates and slower IT investment are presented as more plausible explanations for weaker tech hiring than AI as of 2026-04-18.
- Outsourcing is a concrete cost-cutting channel in the piece, with U.S. imports of cloud-computing and data-storage services more than doubling from 2021 to 2024.
- Tech labor demand is not collapsing uniformly: the article says tech-related occupations rose from 3.6% to 3.7% of workers between November 2022 and the article date.
- The durable implication is that AI may change where tech workers are employed before it sharply reduces total demand for tech skills.

## Why it matters

The article is useful because it separates a noisy AI narrative from more ordinary labor-market drivers. For anyone building AI products or hiring technical teams, the key lesson is that apparent layoffs in major tech firms do not automatically prove AI substitution. The piece ties the slowdown to a post-pandemic correction, tighter monetary conditions, weaker IT capital spending, and outsourcing, all of which are directly relevant to staffing and cost planning. It also grounds the argument with cross-country employment data and cites surveys suggesting AI had essentially zero employment impact over the prior three years, which makes the claim more than a hand-wavy opinion. The practical reading is that as of 2026-04-18, AI tools may be improving fast enough to matter for some coding tasks, but the article argues that the employment effects were still limited at the time covered. For product teams, that means labor substitution claims should be tested against actual adoption and budget data rather than assumed from model capability alone. The article does not substantively discuss customer support, voice, meetings, or back-office automation, so there is no direct operational takeaway in those areas. Actionable as of 2026-04-18, the piece supports monitoring AI-labor claims rather than treating them as settled.

## Limitations / open questions

The argument relies on aggregate employment and adoption data, so it cannot cleanly separate AI effects from cyclicals at the firm level. The article itself says early AI tools were too primitive, which raises the question of when, exactly, the transition to meaningful substitution begins. It cites one survey finding essentially zero employment impact over three years, but surveys may miss lagged effects or sector-specific disruption. The outsourcing evidence is suggestive, but the article does not quantify how much of the hiring slowdown it explains versus interest rates or post-pandemic normalization. It also does not show causal identification for the claim that AI is not yet the main driver. The piece says tech skills are spreading into other sectors, but it does not tell us whether those jobs are equivalent in quality, pay, or stability.

## Contradictions / unverified claims

The strongest claim to scrutinize is that AI is not yet to blame, because the article’s argument depends heavily on timing and inference rather than direct causal measurement. It acknowledges that Claude Code made replacement of software engineers remotely plausible only from February 2025, which leaves a short window for observed employment effects and makes the conclusion necessarily provisional. The piece also leans on broad employment shares and missing-job estimates, which can overstate structural change if business cycles are driving part of the movement. Still, the skepticism cuts both ways: AI boosters may be overreading capability demos, while the article may understate how quickly adoption can move once tools become useful. On balance, the evidence supports caution more than certainty.
