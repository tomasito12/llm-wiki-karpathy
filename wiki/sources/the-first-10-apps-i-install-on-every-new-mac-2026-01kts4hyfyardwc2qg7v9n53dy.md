# The First 10 Apps I Install on Every New Mac (2026)

This is a “what I install first on a new Mac” list. The author is not trying to cover every useful utility; they are showing the small set of apps that make up their actual daily work surface. The interesting part is the order: browser, password manager, email, calendar, AI assistant, and then the tools for screenshots, writing, tasks, and terminal work. That order matters because many later apps depend on the earlier ones being set up first. It is useful if you want a practical Mac setup, but most picks are personal and opinionated rather than universally best. The article also makes a point of costs, so you can see which tools are free, subscription-based, or one-time purchases.

## Key insights

- Install order is treated as a dependency graph: browser, password manager, email, and calendar come first because later setup depends on them.
- The author’s setup separates “workbench” apps from background utilities; the former are where they spend 10+ hours a day, while the latter are gap-fillers.
- Several recommendations are explicitly value-based preferences, not broad defaults: Mimestream for Gmail-heavy users, Things 3 for solo operators, and iA Writer for AI-free writing.
- Claude Desktop is justified almost entirely by the global keyboard shortcut, showing that small interaction costs can matter more than model differences in daily use.
- The article gives a concrete cost model for a premium Mac workflow, with year-one and recurring costs separated from one-time purchases.

## Why it matters

The piece is useful because it turns a vague “Mac setup” question into a concrete workflow stack with explicit sequencing, cost, and tradeoffs. For AI engineering readers, the most durable idea is not the exact app list but the dependency-aware way the author composes a machine: get identity, communication, and context tools in place before adding specialized work apps. That is a practical pattern for any productivity stack built around browser-based tools, auth, calendar, note-taking, and AI access. The roundup also highlights where product quality is judged by friction, not feature count: Mimestream wins on Gmail shortcuts and native behavior, Claude Desktop wins on keyboard summonability, and iA Writer wins by refusing extra layers. The cost breakdown is also operationally useful because it separates one-time purchases from recurring subscriptions and shows that a “lean” pro setup can still exceed $400 in year one. The strongest signal for tool builders is that small interaction wins, like global hotkeys or shortcut parity, are treated as worth paying for. The service and meeting angle is limited but real: Soda is positioned as a meeting-context capture layer, and the author prefers it over transcript-focused note-takers for PM follow-up. Actionable as of 2026-05-17, but best read as a personal workflow template rather than evidence of a broader market direction.

## Limitations / open questions

This is a single-person preference roundup, so it has no benchmarks, no comparative methodology, and no evidence that the chosen apps outperform alternatives beyond the author’s experience. Pricing is reported as of the publication date, but ongoing costs can change, especially subscriptions and app-store pricing. Several picks depend on specific ecosystems or habits: Google Calendar, Gmail, Apple devices, solo task management, and frequent terminal use. The article does not test long-term reliability, security posture, data retention behavior, or migration cost if a vendor changes direction. The meeting-context claims for Soda are based on workflow fit, not on measured accuracy, coverage, or privacy review. The piece also assumes the reader values native Mac apps and keyboard-driven interaction, which may not hold for teams that need collaboration-heavy or cross-platform tools.

## Contradictions / unverified claims

The article is candid about subjectivity, but it still leans on personal comfort as evidence for superiority in several places. Claims like “best Gmail client” or “best Google Calendar client” are strong relative statements without a public benchmark. The argument that install order matters is plausible, yet it is presented as self-evident rather than demonstrated with failure cases beyond anecdote. Soda is described as broader than meeting note-takers, but the differentiation remains qualitative and not independently validated. The author’s rejection of AI features in iA Writer is a clear product stance, but it is a preference argument, not proof that AI-assisted editing is harmful. Overall, skepticism should stay moderate: the list is coherent and honest about tradeoffs, but it is not generalizable evidence.
