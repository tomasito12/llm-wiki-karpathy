# AI increases human workload through supervision and exception handling

## Interview Insight

### Summary

Dan Shipper argues that automation does not remove human work; it shifts humans into oversight, correction, and decision-making roles. He says models make "yesterday's human competence cheap," which commoditizes routine output and leaves people to define, verify, and improve what the model produces. He frames this as the core paradox of AI-heavy work as of 2026-05-24: more automation can mean more work for humans, not less.

### Why It Matters

This is a durable operating model for teams adopting AI agents as of 2026-05-24. It suggests the main productivity gain comes from accelerating routine production while preserving human judgment for review and direction, rather than expecting end-to-end automation. For AI engineering and service automation, the implication is that staffing, QA, and escalation paths remain central even when model capability improves.

### Operational Relevance

Design workflows assuming human review is part of the system, not a temporary crutch. Build for approval, exception handling, and rapid repair, because agents can generate more output than teams can safely absorb. The practical architecture is supervised automation, not fully autonomous automation.

### Service Automation Relevance

Support automation should be designed for human-in-the-loop escalation and recovery, because the transcript argues every agent needs a human overseeing it. This applies to chatbots, voicebots, and back-office automation where reliability depends on monitoring, repair, and clear ownership.
